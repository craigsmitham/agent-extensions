# Knowledge management overview

Knowledge management makes durable understanding available to the people and
systems that need it. Its object is not merely documents. Knowledge may be
expressed as prose, code, tests, schemas, instructions, recorded evidence, or
live operational state. Good management places each claim in the form and
location capable of keeping it accurate.

The discipline has four connected responsibilities:

1. **Authority:** identify which source owns a claim.
2. **Discovery:** make the source findable without copying it everywhere.
3. **Trust:** expose enough provenance and currency to judge reliance.
4. **Lifecycle:** revise or remove knowledge as its subject changes.

These responsibilities reinforce one another. A well-written document is not
useful if nobody can find it, a searchable document is unsafe if its authority
is unclear, and an authoritative source becomes misleading when nobody owns its
maintenance.

## Non-responsibilities

Knowledge management does not determine whether an underlying claim is true;
the subject's accountable people and evidence do. It does not replace
documentation craft, records management, work tracking, observability, or the
executable contracts of a system. It decides how those sources participate in a
coherent knowledge environment.

The goal is not maximum capture. It is the smallest body of durable knowledge
that readers can find, trust, and maintain.
