# Jobs to Be Done

A Job to Be Done describes progress that a person or organization seeks in a
particular circumstance. It focuses product inquiry on why people change or
choose, not only on who they are or which feature they request.[^christensen-know-customers-jobs]

A useful job statement includes:

- the actor or decision-maker whose progress matters;
- the circumstance that makes the progress relevant;
- the progress or outcome sought; and
- important functional, social, or emotional dimensions when evidence supports
  them.

For example: “When a support incident crosses team boundaries, restore shared
understanding quickly enough to make a safe ownership decision.” The statement
does not prescribe a dashboard, chatbot, workflow engine, or organizational
structure.

## Jobs are not solutions or tasks

A feature request embeds a proposed solution. A task describes an activity. A
job describes progress that may persist while products and methods change.
Keeping that distinction allows several solutions to compete and makes
non-consumption, workarounds, and alternatives visible.

Jobs are also not interchangeable with Needs. A Need describes a desired state
or problem; a Job frames progress in circumstances. They often illuminate one
another, but forcing every Need into a Job statement—or the reverse—can erase
useful evidence.

## Evidence and forces

Investigate actual behavior around a decision or change:

- what pushed someone away from the current situation;
- what pulled them toward an alternative;
- which anxieties discouraged change; and
- which habits or constraints sustained the status quo.

Interviews are strongest when anchored in specific past events rather than
abstract preferences. Combine them with observations, usage, switching,
support, commercial, or operational evidence as appropriate. Treat a job as a
hypothesis until evidence supports it, and keep plausible competing
interpretations visible.

## Related views

A job map decomposes progress into outcome-oriented steps without committing
to a solution. A journey describes an experience over time with a current or
proposed process. A use case describes interactions and outcomes at a system
boundary. These views can complement a Job to Be Done, but none is a lossless
substitute for the others.

[^christensen-know-customers-jobs]: Christensen and coauthors frame jobs around
    the progress customers seek in particular circumstances.
