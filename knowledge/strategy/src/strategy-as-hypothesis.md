# Strategy as hypothesis

A strategy makes claims about an uncertain future: selected participants will
value an offering or outcome, a proposed way to win will influence their
behavior, required capabilities can be built, and the resulting system will
remain viable. These are hypotheses even when they are not written in
experimental language.

Make the consequential assumptions explicit. For each serious alternative,
ask what would have to be true for it to work and what evidence would
distinguish it from the others. Evidence is most useful when competing choices
predict different observations.

Strategic evidence may include customer behavior, economics, capability
performance, competitor response, regulation, partner participation, or other
context-specific signals. A metric is not strategic merely because executives
review it; it matters when it tests an assumption in the choice system.

A Wardley map is one way to externalize landscape hypotheses. Its dependency
links, component positions, movement, and inertia remain claims to test rather
than facts established by coordinates. When a disputed placement would change
the strategy, preserve the disagreement or compare alternative
maps.[^strategy-wardley]

Revision is not failure. New evidence may require changing one choice and then
reconciling the rest of the cascade. Conversely, reacting to every short-term
signal produces tactics without a stable hypothesis. The appropriate cadence
follows the rate at which material assumptions can be tested or invalidated.

The purpose of treating strategy as hypothesis is disciplined learning, not
false certainty or endless experimentation. Choices remain commitments until
evidence justifies reconsidering them.

[^strategy-wardley]: Wardley maps externalize time-sensitive landscape
    hypotheses; they do not turn estimates into facts.
