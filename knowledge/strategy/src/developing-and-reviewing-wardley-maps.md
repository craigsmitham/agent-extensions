# Developing and reviewing Wardley maps

Use a Wardley map when the strategic decision depends on seeing a user-anchored
dependency chain and how its components may evolve. Use another view when the
question is primarily financial, organizational, architectural, operational,
or about process sequence.

## Develop a map

### 1. Frame the question

Record:

- the strategic question or decision;
- the selected user or stakeholder and their need;
- scope and important exclusions;
- the observation date and owner; and
- the review event or date.

A map without a question tends to become an inventory. A map without a user
need lacks the perspective that gives vertical visibility meaning.

### 2. Build the dependency chain

Start at the user need and ask what is required to satisfy it, then what those
components depend on. Include activities, practices, information, knowledge,
capabilities, suppliers, and technology when relevant. Do not force components
to align with the current organization or system structure.

Keep the first map small enough to challenge. Expand only where an omitted
dependency could change the decision.

### 3. Position evolution with evidence

Estimate each component's evolution from ubiquity and certainty, not age or
delivery status. Record the evidence, confidence, and material disagreement
behind positions that affect the decision. If two plausible placements imply
different action, preserve both hypotheses instead of averaging them.

### 4. Add movement, climate, and inertia

Mark expected or intended movement as a hypothesis. Note external patterns
that could reshape the landscape and sources of inertia that may resist change.
Distinguish observed constraint from speculation and identify what evidence
would alter the claim.[^strategy-wardley]

### 5. Generate alternatives

Use the map to prompt alternatives such as investing, differentiating,
standardizing, sourcing, migrating, preserving optionality, or changing a
dependency. For each serious alternative, ask:

- what would have to be true;
- how it connects to where to play and how to win;
- which capabilities and systems it requires;
- what it excludes; and
- what evidence could disconfirm it.

The map improves the option set and the assumptions behind it. The strategy's
choice authority makes the commitment.[^strategy-choice]

### 6. Record the handoff

Link the selected strategy to the mapped assumptions it relies on. Project
accepted consequences into the discipline that owns them—such as product,
capability, risk, architecture, operations, or work management—without treating
the map as their canonical artifact.

## Review a map

Check that:

- the question, user, need, scope, date, owner, and review trigger are explicit;
- every component participates in a relevant dependency chain;
- visibility is from the chosen user's perspective;
- evolution is not being used as time, maturity, progress, or quality;
- consequential positions and movement have evidence and confidence;
- inertia is investigated rather than labeled irrational;
- alternative maps or dissent remain visible where they change the decision;
- strategic options are connected to an integrated choice system; and
- downstream artifacts own accepted commitments and consequences.

Refresh or retire a map when its question is resolved, its evidence expires, a
material dependency changes, expected movement occurs, or the governing
strategy changes. A stale map presented without its observation date is more
misleading than no map.

[^strategy-wardley]: The Wardley Mapping explanation defines the map grammar,
    evolution model, strategy cycle, inertia, and limits used by this guide.
[^strategy-choice]: The choice cascade retains authority for integrated
    strategic commitments; a map informs but does not replace those choices.
