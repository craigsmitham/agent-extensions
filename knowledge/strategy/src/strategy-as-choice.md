# Strategy as choice

A strategic choice commits resources and behavior to one path while excluding
credible alternatives. If every customer, market, capability, and opportunity
remains equally important, no choice has been made.

Choices are strategic when they answer a connected set of questions:

- What result are we trying to create?
- Where will we participate?
- How will we succeed there rather than merely be present?
- What must we be unusually capable of doing?
- What systems will sustain those capabilities and choices?

The answers must reinforce one another. Roger Martin emphasizes that the
difficulty of the strategy choice cascade lies in integration, not filling five
independent boxes.[^martin-integration] A plausible choice can still be wrong when it
requires incompatible capabilities or undermines the proposed advantage.

Tradeoffs are evidence of choice, but sacrifice alone is not strategy. The
excluded alternatives should make the selected system clearer and more
coherent. “Focus” without an account of how the focus creates advantage is only
reduced scope.

Plans sequence actions after choices have been made. Goals state desired
results. Neither explains why this particular system of action should succeed.

[^martin-integration]: Martin describes fit and mutual reinforcement across the
    cascade as the central integrative challenge.
