# How to design a checklist

Bring an instance of the omission and the procedure or agreement that
establishes what should have happened. In this example an exhibition
registrar asks for “a packing checklist” after a signed receipt stayed
on her desk. Reconstruct the handover with the registrar and the courier.
The crate was already packed. The opportunity to catch the missing
receipt was on the trolley, just before the courier took the crate.

## Choose the moment and the people

Write down who is present, what each can observe, and who can act if the
answer is wrong. Consider whether changing the equipment or procedure
would remove the opportunity for error altogether. If a receipt can only
be printed at another site, a new prompt at the trolley will be too late.

At this venue the registrar can hold a crate, and the courier carries its
document wallet. Both know the procedure. Put the prompt on the trolley
and have them use it before releasing each crate. In **read-do**, someone reads a prompt
and then performs the action. In **do-confirm**, they compare completed
work with a criterion. In **challenge-response**, one participant calls
an item and another reports the observed state. That exchange can support
either sequence. Match the interaction to
what the team can actually see, say, and handle at that moment.[^flight]

## Write one item you could observe in use

Get each consequential criterion and exception from the person or record
that governs it. “Paperwork OK?” invites a nod. Under the venue's handover
agreement, the crate ID and the signed receipt have to match. Try this
extract on a card clipped to the trolley.

> **Before each crate leaves**
>
> - Registrar reads the ID from the crate. Courier reads the ID from the
>   signed receipt in the wallet. Record the matching ID on the dispatch
>   sheet.
> - If the receipt is missing or the IDs differ, leave the crate on the
>   trolley. The registrar resolves the discrepancy and repeats the
>   comparison before releasing it.

The recording step follows the observation. For another task, a mark or
a spoken response may be enough. Split an item if two parts can be
completed independently and the user needs to know which remains. Use the
words people recognize on the equipment. Try reading the actual card
where it will hang, with the lighting, noise, language, and available
hands the users will have. If someone can miss a quiet reply or cannot
distinguish two colors, try an ID written on the dispatch sheet or a
short status word that both participants can read.

## Try a case that could fool the checklist

Hand the team a matching pair, then try two plausible-looking receipts
with their crate IDs swapped. Interrupt one handover midway. Observe
where the people look and what they do when they return. Could two people
say “yes” without either reading the receipt? Could a half-completed line
be mistaken for a released crate? Revise that item or the placement of the
card and repeat the case.

For a checklist adapted from another site, identify what has to remain
true for each item to work. If the new venue never has both people at the
trolley, design and try a different exchange. The old venue's successful
rehearsal cannot settle how the new exchange works.

## Decide what would justify keeping it

State the claim and collect evidence that could change your mind. For
this card, compare mismatched or missing receipts reaching the destination
before and after introduction, along with delays and workarounds. Record
changes in the kinds or numbers of shipments that could affect the
comparison. A walkthrough can expose an ambiguous prompt. A tally of
signatures can show recorded use. Outcomes require their own evidence.[^trial]

For a reusable draft, leave a short maintainer note beside the working
card. Identify the procedure that warrants the items, who can settle an
open criterion, which cases still need observation, and what result would
lead the owner to revise or retire it. Label untried designs as candidates.
Keep these notes available to the next maintainer without making the
operator read them on every use.

Give the card an owner and a way to identify its revision. Ask the owner
to revisit it after a missed handover or a change to the dispatch
agreement. If the next process removes separate paper receipts entirely,
retire the card and update the trolley instructions.
