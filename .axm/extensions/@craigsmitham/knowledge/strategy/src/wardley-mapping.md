# Wardley mapping

A Wardley map is a strategic model of a value chain anchored in a user or
stakeholder need. It places dependencies by visibility to that need and by
their stage of evolution, then uses movement, constraints, and inertia to
reason about action.[^wardley-maps]

It is not a geographic map, roadmap, capability hierarchy, process diagram, or
static component architecture. Its value comes from making a strategic
hypothesis discussable:

- **Need:** whose need anchors the map and what outcome matters to them?
- **Value chain:** which dependencies are required, and which are more or less
  visible to the need?
- **Evolution:** are dependencies novel, custom-built, productized, or
  commodity/utility-like in this context?
- **Movement:** which dependencies are likely or intended to evolve, and what
  forces drive that change?
- **Inertia and constraints:** what resists movement, creates risk, or changes
  the feasible play?
- **Action:** what should be built, bought, standardized, differentiated,
  decoupled, measured, or watched?

## Treat the map as a hypothesis

Record the scope, date, author or accountable owner, evidence, contested
positions, and review trigger. Position is contextual: a component can be
commodity-like for one organization and novel for another because knowledge,
market availability, regulation, and user need differ.

Avoid false precision. The map's axes support comparison and reasoning, not a
measurement claim merely because an element is placed at a coordinate. Make
the consequential uncertainty visible and name observations that would move an
element or invalidate the proposed action.

## Connect maps to decisions

A useful map produces choices and learning obligations. Distinguish:

1. what the map currently hypothesizes;
2. what strategic action is chosen because of it;
3. what architectural, organizational, sourcing, or investment consequence is
   accepted; and
4. what evidence will cause review.

Reuse canonical capability, context, structural, or external-system names when
the same elements appear elsewhere. The map supplies strategic relationships
and evolutionary context; it does not become a duplicate authority for their
domain meaning or architecture.

## Common failure modes

- Mapping internal technology without an anchoring need.
- Treating a dependency graph as a map while omitting evolution and movement.
- Using evolution labels as praise or criticism rather than context.
- Converting a workshop snapshot into permanent fact without evidence or a
  freshness boundary.
- Jumping from position to build-versus-buy conclusions without considering
  constraints, inertia, doctrine, and the organization's actual choices.
- Maintaining a second inventory of architecture elements instead of linking
  to their canonical owners.
