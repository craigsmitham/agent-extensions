# Validation and lifecycle

Match evidence to the exact claim. Surface quality, source traceability, user
preference, completion, task performance, downstream outcomes, learning, and
durability are different evidence levels.

## Build a claim ladder

1. **Content and fit:** critical items trace to authoritative evidence, task
   analysis, incidents or defects, and an explicit purpose; neighboring aids
   were considered.
2. **Interpretation and usability:** representative users can select, navigate,
   understand, execute, and recover in realistic conditions, including access
   needs and interruptions.
3. **Observed mechanism:** use occurs as designed; roles, verification,
   correction, speaking up, and escalation are observable rather than inferred
   from recorded completion.
4. **Comparative task outcome:** the checklist is compared with actual current
   practice or a feasible alternative on the claimed errors, defects, timing,
   coordination, decisions, or learning.
5. **Harms and distribution:** delay, overload, misselection, workarounds,
   false assurance, unequal access, and displaced controls are measured.
6. **Durability and transfer:** effects persist after coaching, across relevant
   users and settings, after adaptation, and as work changes.

Evidence at one rung supports no stronger claim automatically. Simulation can
establish simulated task behavior without establishing rare real-world harm
reduction. Reporting completeness does not establish truth. Process adherence
does not prove a downstream outcome.

## Define a purpose-fit validation plan

Record:

- the claim and unit of judgment;
- population, workflow, environment, version, and exclusions;
- actual comparator or alternative;
- representative scenarios, including interruption and exception cases;
- measures, observation method, and baseline;
- access, expertise, language, role, and device strata that could change use;
- unintended effects and disconfirming thresholds;
- implementation conditions such as training, resources, authority, coaching,
  and monitoring; and
- how null, conflicting, missing, or unavailable evidence remains visible.

Prefer direct observation or trustworthy interaction evidence to self-report
where ritual completion is plausible. Use inter-reviewer agreement when the
checklist asks reviewers to interpret or score evidence. For learning, measure
delayed, preferably unassisted retention and transfer.

When comparing review or inspection methods, measure overlap and unique
findings by condition where they can distinguish complementary coverage from
duplicated effort. Adjudicate whether findings are valid and material; a larger
raw finding count is not automatically a better outcome.

## Seek disconfirmation

A checklist's quality claim is weakened or contradicted when:

- critical content is missing, stale, or unsupported;
- users misinterpret items or select the wrong checklist or branch;
- it delays essential action or adds irrelevant work;
- checked completion diverges from observed execution or required state;
- process adherence improves but the claimed outcome does not;
- workarounds, hierarchy, or missing resources defeat the mechanism;
- outcomes differ materially across access or user groups;
- effects vanish after implementation support ends;
- local adaptation removes a core mechanism; or
- a feasible alternative performs better.

Do not reinterpret null or adverse evidence as success by silently narrowing
the original claim.

## Govern the lifecycle

Every maintained checklist should identify:

- accountable owner and approver where one exists;
- content sources, provenance, creation date, version, and revision rationale;
- invariant content or mechanism and locally adaptable form;
- training, resources, authority, and companion artifacts required for use;
- field-test status and the evidence actually established;
- feedback, incident, defect, process, equipment, evidence, and accessibility
  signals that trigger review;
- how adaptations are documented and revalidated; and
- conditions for replacement, suspension, or retirement.

Textual stability is not fitness. A checklist can become stale when the task,
users, evidence, resources, incentives, tools, or surrounding workflow change.
Completion rate alone can preserve the appearance of use while attentive
practice decays.
